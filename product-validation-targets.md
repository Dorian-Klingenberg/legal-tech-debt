# Legal Tech Debt Product Validation Targets

Date: 2026-06-06
Status: Working targeting note
Purpose: Identify who should see the product first, what they should see, and what question each conversation should answer.

---

## Short Answer

Do **not** start by pitching a whole insurance carrier.

Start with people who already understand one of these pains:

- policy form compliance,
- state-by-state insurance regulation,
- claims disputes caused by policy language,
- regulatory examinations or market conduct reviews,
- insurance document review workflows.

The first objective is not a sale. It is to learn whether the product is pointing at a real budgeted pain.

---

## Best First Audience

### 1. Insurance compliance professionals

This is probably the best first category.

Why they fit:

- They live close to policy forms, state variations, filings, approvals, and regulatory interpretations.
- They can recognize whether a finding is operationally meaningful.
- They may know who inside a carrier would own the problem.

What to show:

- one sanitized report section,
- one or two real findings,
- the evidence trail,
- the current limitation: two Kentucky carriers, five policy-layer smells, prototype stage.

What to ask:

- "Would this have saved you time in a real compliance review?"
- "Would this be more useful before filing, after filing, during claims review, or during remediation?"
- "Who would pay attention to this inside a carrier?"
- "What would make you distrust it?"

Possible places to find them:

- Association of Insurance Compliance Professionals (AICP): https://www.aicp.net/
- LinkedIn searches for "insurance compliance", "policy form compliance", "regulatory compliance insurance", "product compliance insurance".

### 2. Former insurance regulators or regulatory examiners

This is the best credibility category.

Why they fit:

- They understand what regulators care about.
- They can tell whether a finding sounds like a real regulatory concern or just an interesting document issue.
- They may help separate "legally interesting" from "commercially urgent."

What to show:

- a sample finding with source citations,
- the detector/reviewer workflow at a high level,
- the uncertainty labels.

What to ask:

- "Would this be recognizable as a real regulatory issue?"
- "Would a department of insurance care, or is this too internal to the carrier?"
- "What would a carrier need to see before acting on this?"
- "What language should I avoid so this does not sound accusatory or naive?"

Possible places to find them:

- Insurance Regulatory Examiners Society (IRES): https://www.go-ires.org/
- IRES Foundation events: https://www.ires-foundation.org/
- NAIC professional-development and regulatory education ecosystem: https://content.naic.org/
- LinkedIn searches for "former insurance regulator", "market conduct examiner", "insurance examiner", "state insurance department".

### 3. Insurance regulatory lawyers

This is the best risk-and-positioning category.

Why they fit:

- They know how carriers interpret policy language, filings, endorsements, and claims exposure.
- They can tell whether the report should be positioned as compliance QA, litigation-risk triage, regulatory remediation, or policy-form review support.

What to show:

- a sanitized sample report,
- the workflow diagram,
- the quality-control limits,
- the exact claim you want to make about the product.

What to ask:

- "Is this framed in a way a carrier could safely discuss?"
- "Where would this fit in existing review workflows?"
- "Would this be useful to counsel, compliance, product, or claims?"
- "What would make this look like unauthorized legal advice?"

### 4. Insurance product/forms people

This may be the closest path to eventual buyer pain.

Why they fit:

- They deal with policy language before it becomes claims trouble.
- They may know how much work goes into form maintenance, endorsements, and state variation.

What to show:

- a finding that connects policy wording to a practical filing/compliance risk,
- a before/after view of how the report reduces review time,
- a state-specific example.

What to ask:

- "Would this catch something your current workflow might miss?"
- "Would you use this before filing or during form refresh?"
- "What systems or vendors already touch this process?"
- "How often do these reviews happen?"

LinkedIn searches:

- "insurance product compliance"
- "policy forms insurance"
- "forms analyst insurance"
- "insurance product manager homeowners"
- "property casualty compliance"

### 5. Claims coverage people

This is useful, but probably not the first audience.

Why they fit:

- They see where policy wording creates disputes.
- They can tell whether a finding has downstream claims significance.

Why not first:

- They may care more about claim handling than pre-filing policy hygiene.
- They may see the product as interesting but not theirs to buy.

Good first question:

> "Does this kind of policy-language issue ever turn into claim confusion, dispute cost, or escalation?"

## Who Not To Start With

### Whole carriers

Too large, too political, too many gatekeepers.

Start with one human inside or adjacent to a carrier, not the company.

### Procurement

They cannot validate the pain. They only enter after a buyer already cares.

### General AI investors

They may pull the conversation toward scale, moat, and pitch polish before the product has buyer proof.

### Random attorneys

Only useful if they understand insurance regulation, insurance coverage, compliance, or product filings.

### Grant writers

Not product validators.

---

## First Ten Conversation Targets

The first ten should be deliberately mixed:

1. Two insurance compliance professionals.
2. Two former regulators or market-conduct examiners.
3. Two insurance regulatory or coverage lawyers.
4. Two insurance product/forms people.
5. One claims coverage person.
6. One insurtech/product operator who sells into carriers.

The goal is not consensus. The goal is pattern detection.

If three or more of the first ten say, "Yes, this is a real workflow pain, and the likely owner is X," that is a strong signal to keep going.

If most say, "Interesting, but I do not know who owns this," then the product may need a narrower wedge.

## Outreach Order

Do not begin with "regulatory professionals" as one broad category. Split the market into three lanes:

1. **Buyer-workflow lane:** carrier-side compliance, product/forms, policy administration, and claims coverage people.
2. **Credibility lane:** former regulators, market-conduct examiners, and insurance regulatory lawyers.
3. **Go-to-market lane:** insurtech operators, consultants, and people who sell compliance or document tools into carriers.

The first outreach batch should include all three lanes, but the highest priority is the buyer-workflow lane.

### First five people to try

1. A carrier-side insurance compliance professional.
2. A policy/forms or product compliance person at a property/casualty carrier.
3. A former market-conduct examiner or former state insurance department person.
4. An insurance regulatory or coverage lawyer.
5. An insurtech operator who sells to compliance, product, legal, or claims teams.

### How to contact them

Use a short advisory-interview request, not a product pitch.

Template:

> Hi [Name], I am building a source-traceable review workflow for insurance policy and regulatory document risk. I am not selling anything right now; I am trying to understand whether this maps to a real compliance, forms, or claims workflow. Could I show you one sanitized example and ask for 15-20 minutes of feedback on where, if anywhere, this would fit?

Follow-up if they respond:

> Thank you. I will keep it tight: one prototype example, what the tool found, how the evidence is shown, and three questions about fit. I am not asking you to review code or endorse anything.

### What to ask on the call

Ask these in this order:

1. "Is this a real problem in your world, or just an interesting artifact?"
2. "Who would own this workflow inside a carrier?"
3. "When would it matter: before filing, after filing, during claims, during audits, or during remediation?"
4. "What would make you trust or distrust this kind of report?"
5. "What existing tools, vendors, lawyers, or internal teams already touch this?"
6. "If I wanted three more informed conversations, what job titles should I look for?"

Do not ask for pricing feedback until they first confirm the workflow pain.

### How to know if a conversation worked

Strong signal:

- They name a specific owner or department.
- They describe an existing review process this could improve.
- They ask to see another example.
- They suggest a specific colleague or job title.
- They say what evidence or QA would make it credible.

Weak signal:

- They only say "interesting."
- They cannot name who would own it.
- They move immediately to general AI concerns.
- They treat it as legal research rather than insurance workflow support.
- They say it would be useful only if someone else already wanted it.

### Two-week starter plan

Week 1:

- Prepare one sanitized sample report.
- Write the 90-second product explanation.
- Identify 20 names across the three lanes.
- Send 10 short outreach messages.

Week 2:

- Hold 3-5 calls if possible.
- Log each conversation by role, workflow owner, trust concern, and suggested next target.
- Revise the sample report only after patterns appear, not after every single opinion.

The goal is not to convince the first few people. The goal is to find out what job title says, "Yes, this is mine."

## What To Show First

Use a **sanitized sample report**, not the whole repo.

Include:

- a one-paragraph product description,
- the current scope,
- one strong finding,
- the evidence trail,
- what the tool did and what a human reviewed,
- known limitations,
- three questions you want answered.

Avoid:

- full source code,
- claims that the product is exhaustive,
- claims that the report is legal advice,
- implying that two Kentucky carriers prove national market demand,
- pricing claims before they validate the pain.

## How To Share Without Losing Control

The product can be shown safely if the share is layered.

The key distinction:

- **Safe to share early:** the problem, a sanitized output, the evidence style, the limits, and the validation questions.
- **Do not share early:** source code, detector internals, prompts/workflows, full corpus strategy, automation scripts, private roadmap, pricing model details, and anything that would let someone reproduce the workflow quickly.

### Layer 0: private ownership cleanup

Before serious external sharing, clarify:

- whether the current work belongs to you personally, the Canadian corporation, or both,
- whether the 51% owner has any claim to the project,
- whether the code, reports, domain, brand, corpus notes, and methods are assigned somewhere,
- whether a new U.S. entity would need a license or assignment if used later.

This does not need to be perfect before every coffee chat, but it should be answered before pilots, grants, paid work, or sharing under another company's vendor process.

### Layer 1: public description

This is safe for emails, LinkedIn messages, and casual introductions:

> Legal Tech Debt is a source-traceable review workflow for finding insurance policy and regulatory document risks. The current prototype tests a small Kentucky homeowners-policy corpus and reports findings with supporting source evidence and human-review notes.

This explains the product without giving away the engine.

### Layer 2: sanitized sample report

Use this for first validation calls.

Include:

- one or two findings,
- source citations,
- a short note on how evidence was reviewed,
- explicit prototype limits,
- no private code,
- no full detector taxonomy,
- no full corpus procurement strategy,
- no "secret sauce" implementation details.

Add a footer:

> Confidential evaluation draft. Shared for feedback only. Do not forward without permission.

That footer is not magic legal armor, but it establishes that you are treating the material as controlled.

### Layer 3: controlled walkthrough

Use screen share, not file transfer, when the person is not yet trusted.

Show:

- the report output,
- the evidence chain,
- the human-review workflow,
- a high-level workflow diagram.

Do not show:

- repository structure,
- scripts,
- prompts,
- detector definitions,
- raw working notes,
- source acquisition details.

### Layer 4: NDA or professional engagement

Use this only when a deeper party needs more detail.

Good reasons:

- a potential pilot customer wants to evaluate internals,
- a lawyer needs to review IP or risk positioning,
- a technical partner needs to inspect architecture,
- a grant or research partner needs methods detail.

Bad reasons:

- a first intro call,
- a curiosity conversation,
- someone who says "send me everything and I will take a look",
- anyone asking for ownership, exclusivity, or broad rights before giving specific value.

### Layer 5: pilot terms

Before a real pilot, define:

- who owns pre-existing IP,
- who owns new improvements,
- whether customer documents can train or improve the system,
- whether outputs can be used as examples,
- confidentiality,
- liability limits,
- whether the output is legal advice or decision support,
- whether the customer can share reports internally or externally.

Do not run a meaningful pilot on handshake terms if it uses private customer documents or gives the customer deep access to the workflow.

## What Can Actually Be Lost

The vague fear is "someone steals it." Break that into parts:

- **The abstract idea:** hard to protect by itself.
- **The code:** protected as expression, but keep it private.
- **The method/workflow:** protect as trade secret unless and until an IP lawyer says patent strategy makes sense.
- **The brand/name:** can be protected through trademark strategy if it becomes commercial.
- **The report format:** partly expression, partly know-how; share only sanitized examples.
- **The corpus/source map:** valuable operational knowledge; do not publish the full map early.
- **The buyer learning:** protect by moving faster and keeping a validation log.
- **The relationships:** strongest practical protection; build them directly.

Sources:

- U.S. Copyright Office FAQ: copyright protects software and other original expression, but not the underlying idea itself.  
  https://www.copyright.gov/help/faq/faq-protect.html

- WIPO trade secret overview: trade secrets stay protected while commercially valuable information remains confidential and protected by reasonable measures.  
  https://www.wipo.int/en/web/trade-secrets/protection

- USPTO provisional patent application overview: a provisional application can establish an official U.S. filing date.  
  https://www.uspto.gov/patents-getting-started/patent-basics/types-patent-applications/provisional-application-patent

## Trust Rule

Give each person only enough to answer one question.

- Compliance person: "Is this a real workflow pain?"
- Former regulator: "Is this a real regulatory concern?"
- Lawyer: "Is this framed safely and credibly?"
- Product/forms person: "Would this fit before filing or form refresh?"
- Claims person: "Does this kind of issue create downstream claim cost?"
- Insurtech operator: "Who buys this and how does procurement work?"

You are not asking them to bless the whole company. You are asking them to correct the map.

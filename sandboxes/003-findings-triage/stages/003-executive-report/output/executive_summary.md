## Industry-Wide Patterns

*Patterns appearing in both carriers independently carry the strongest signal — they reflect industry drafting conventions, not isolated filing choices.*

### Magic Number / Magic Valuation Terms

**SMELL2-H003** (5 + 5 findings across both carriers)

*Language flagged: "Actual Cash Value", "REPLACEMENT COST", "Replacement Cost"*

In the insurance industry, clarity in policy language is crucial, especially in the terms used for loss settlement. The SMELL2-H003 pattern identifies issues with undefined valuation terms such as 'actual cash value' or 'replacement cost' that lack accompanying computation methodologies or external references in policy documents. This pattern has been observed in both carriers analyzed, with examples of terms like 'market value' being used without clear definitions. The absence of these crucial details makes it problematic for policyholders to comprehend how their property will be appraised following a claim, potentially leading to uncertainty and dissatisfaction with the settlement process.

The practical risk emerging from this gap is significant. When valuation terms are not adequately defined, it fosters disputes between policyholders and insurers about the true value of the property at the time of a claim. This was starkly illustrated by the labor depreciation class-action wave, which began in 2015 and saw claims that carriers failed to transparently disclose labor cost depreciation methods in their policy language, resulting in underpayments amounting to hundreds of millions industry-wide. Another instance is the $15.6 million State Farm settlement over ambiguous 'actual cash value' calculations. Such cases underscore how lack of clarity in defining key valuation terms can lead not only to disputes but also to heightened regulatory scrutiny and substantial financial repercussions for insurers.

### Regulatory Mapping Smells

**SMELL5-H004** (1 + 4 findings across both carriers)

The SMELL5-H004 pattern refers to a notable absence in the regulatory mapping of rate-setting and premium-computation filings, where the basis for these rates is not clearly cited against the relevant Kentucky statutes, administrative regulations, or Department of Insurance bulletins. This absence means that the regulatory authority—essentially the foundation that justifies rate adjustments—is not clearly documented or traceable in the filings analyzed. This issue presents in the analysis of both carriers, with one filing detected in one carrier and four in the other. By not clearly referencing applicable legal frameworks, the filings leave open questions about the legitimacy of proposed rate increases.

The practical risk of this pattern lies primarily in potential claim disputes and regulatory scrutiny. If a policyholder challenges a claim payout influenced by these undocumentated rate adjustments, the insurer may find it difficult to defend the rates' validity, potentially resulting in denied claims or insufficient payouts. This scenario mirrors cases like the Florida Office of Insurance Regulation's hurricane aftermath evaluations, which led to $2.575 million in fines due to gaps in regulatory compliance evidence. Similarly, Louisiana's post-storm examinations identified nearly $1 million in proposed fines for similar compliance failures. Thus, the lack of clear regulatory citation in rate filings can expose carriers to significant financial penalties, reflecting broader challenges seen in other states.

**SMELL5-H005** (1 + 1 findings across both carriers)

The SMELL5-H005 pattern identifies policy provisions that assert certain coverages are mandatory without providing a regulatory authority or citation to substantiate the requirement. This pattern was found in the policy documents of both carriers analyzed. The absence of clear regulatory backing introduces ambiguity into the policy terms, as policyholders may not have the necessary information to understand whether specific coverages are genuinely required by law or are merely recommended by the insurer.

This gap in clarity can lead to significant disputes and regulatory challenges. For instance, when a policyholder files a claim expecting coverage they believe is mandatory, only to be denied based on the lack of regulatory requirement, it can result in contentious disputes. This type of regulatory mapping error has tangible consequences, as evidenced by the substantial fines levied following market conduct exams in states like Florida and Louisiana, where similar regulatory compliance failures during hurricane claims led to millions in fines. Without precise regulatory citations, carriers face increased risk of fines and reputational damage, as illustrated by the $750,000 penalty in South Dakota resulting from regulatory mapping omissions.

## Risk Context: What This Costs When It Goes Wrong

*The patterns identified in this analysis are not theoretical. The following public events illustrate the cost range when similar gaps are not caught early.*

**Magic Number / Magic Valuation Terms**

- **Labor depreciation class-action wave** — A wave of class actions beginning around 2015 alleged that carriers were systematically depreciating labor costs when calculating actual cash value — a practice not disclosed or defined in policy language. One Trumbull/Hartford suit alone alleged more than $5 million in underpayments; industry-wide exposure has been estimated in the hundreds of millions.
- **State Farm ACV settlement** — State Farm reached a $15.6 million settlement in a dispute over how actual cash value was calculated on property claims — a direct consequence of undefined ACV methodology in policy language.
- **Kentucky ambiguity and bad-faith exposure** — Project research (Pedicini example) shows that undefined payment terminology in Kentucky policy language has survived as the basis for bad-faith exposure. Kentucky courts treat policy ambiguity against the drafter.

**Calculation Rule Drift / Unversioned Rate Reference**

- **Marshall Fire underinsurance** — A single home insured for $419,000 faced an $850,000 rebuild estimate after the Marshall Fire — a $431,000 gap driven by replacement cost estimates that were not synchronized with actual post-loss reconstruction pricing.
- **Han v. State Farm** — Alleged systematic property underpayment from using lower new-construction pricing rather than reconstruction pricing. Pattern affects entire claim population, not individual cases.

**Regulatory Mapping Smells**

- **Florida OIR hurricane market conduct exams** — Following post-hurricane market conduct examinations, Florida's OIR levied $2.575 million in fines across ten carriers. Findings included missing required notices, claim handling deadline failures, and payment timing violations — all traceable to regulatory mapping gaps in carrier filings.
- **Louisiana hurricane market conduct exams** — Louisiana proposed nearly $1 million in fines following hurricane claims examinations — the same pattern as Florida, driven by missing state-specific regulatory compliance in carrier claim handling.
- **South Dakota Farmers/Foremost market conduct exam** — A single property insurance market conduct examination produced a $750,000 penalty — establishing the low end of the documented fine range for regulatory mapping failures.

## What Forward-Looking Carriers Are Doing

In proactively managing the identified risks, forward-looking carriers will approach these findings as opportunities to enhance clarity and predictability in their policy language. By revisiting provisions involving valuation terms like "actual cash value" and "replacement cost," carriers can eliminate ambiguities that might otherwise lead to disputes or consumer dissatisfaction. These revisions are most effective when they include explicit definitions and incorporate specific methodologies for rate calculations, ensuring that these policies align more closely with consumer expectations and regulatory standards. Additionally, addressing vague timing language in loss settlements with precise and easily understandable conditions will improve transparency and trust with policyholders. By taking these steps, carriers position themselves not merely as compliant but as leaders in customer service and operational excellence.

Beyond individual policy adjustments, the broader value of conducting systematic policy language reviews cannot be understated. Such practices not only preempt potential disputes but also reinforce the carrier's reputation as a dependable and forward-thinking entity in the competitive insurance landscape. Regular evaluations foster a culture of continuous improvement and resilience, enabling carriers to adapt to evolving regulatory landscapes while maintaining robust customer relationships. This commitment to clarity and precision ultimately translates into a competitive advantage, showcasing a proactive stance in managing both operational risks and customer expectations.

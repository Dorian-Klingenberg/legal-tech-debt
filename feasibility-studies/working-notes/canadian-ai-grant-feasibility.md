## Short Answer

Canadian government support is **plausible**, but not automatic.

The project is a better fit for Canadian funding if it is framed as:

> a Canadian AI/regtech product that helps insurance and compliance professionals find source-traceable legal and regulatory document risks earlier.

The project is a weaker fit if it is framed as:

> a solo research project, a generic AI report-writing tool, or a consulting idea without Canadian incorporation, Canadian R&D activity, or buyer validation.

The main gating issue is structure. Most relevant programs require some combination of:

- Canadian incorporation or Canadian registration,
- a CRA business number,
- operations or R&D work carried out in Canada,
- a commercial technology product or service,
- evidence of market potential,
- financial capacity,
- and a plan for economic benefit in Canada.

This means Canadian grants are feasible as part of a commercialization plan, but they are probably not available just because the idea is Canadian or AI-related.

### If the current year is truly zero-activity

If the current tax year has no income and no expenses, the corporation may be relatively cheap to keep clean for a short decision window. But separate three different issues:

1. **Federal annual return**

   This is a Corporations Canada filing, not the same as the T2 tax return. Because the corporation is still active, the annual return is required even if the business is not operating. If it is late, Corporations Canada says the corporation's annual filing status will show as overdue and the corporation will not be able to obtain a Certificate of Compliance.

   Practical implication: if the corporation might be used for grants, pilot conversations, or good-standing checks, filing the overdue annual return may be the cheapest way to preserve optionality while deciding.

2. **T2 tax return**

   CRA requires resident corporations, including inactive corporations, to file a corporation income tax return every tax year even if no tax is payable. If the corporation is kept alive through the current tax year, there will likely still be a T2 filing for that year, even if nil. If it is dissolved, there may be a final T2 up to the dissolution date.

   Practical implication: "no income or expenses" may make the return simpler, but it does not eliminate the filing obligation.

3. **Asset and IP cleanup**

   The biggest threat to a clean zero year is not the AI project. It is the dissolution cleanup itself. If the corporation owns equipment, computers, code, domains, accounts, or other property, transferring those out may create transactions that need to be reflected in books and final filings.

   Practical implication: if the assets were never corporate assets, document that. If they are corporate assets, do not assume the split is bookkeeping-free.

Working recommendation:

> File the overdue federal annual return if the cost is low, then pause dissolution until the asset/IP ownership and grant-option question are checked. If keeping the corporation clean for one more year is cheap, it may be worth preserving. If keeping it clean requires municipal/provincial/accounting/tax work that outweighs the grant option, dissolve it cleanly.

## U.S. Grant Comparison

Because you are also a U.S. citizen living in Michigan, the United States is a real funding path too. The key U.S. mechanism is not a general "AI grant"; it is **SBIR/STTR** and agency-specific innovation funding.

## Main U.S. path: SBIR/STTR

Fit: **Medium, if framed as technical R&D rather than consulting/report writing.**

SBIR/STTR is America's standing small-business R&D funding pathway. It is not specific to AI, but AI projects can qualify when they involve technical innovation and commercialization potential.

Important eligibility signals:

- the applicant must be a U.S. small business,
- the business must be majority owned and controlled by U.S. citizens or permanent residents,
- funded work generally must be performed in the United States,
- the technical lead/principal investigator usually must be primarily employed by the company during the project,
- the project must involve R&D, not routine product development or ordinary consulting.

Your current Canadian corporation is therefore a weak SBIR/STTR applicant candidate under the facts provided. It is Canadian, and it is 51% owned by a Canadian citizen and Canadian resident while you own 49%. Unless the 51% owner is also a U.S. citizen or U.S. permanent resident, that ownership split likely fails the U.S. majority ownership/control test. Even if the ownership question were solved, the applicant would still need to be a U.S. small business and the funded R&D would generally need to be performed in the United States.

That does not eliminate the U.S. grant path. It means the cleaner U.S. path would probably be a separate U.S. entity, with ownership/control, IP rights, work location, and principal-investigator employment structured for SBIR/STTR eligibility from the start.

Why Legal Tech Debt could fit:

- legal evidence extraction and detector calibration are technical R&D problems,
- explainable/legal-source-traceable AI is a real research/commercialization area,
- regulated-document risk analysis could fit AI, compliance, financial-services, or trustworthy AI themes,
- the U.S. insurance market is large and fragmented.

What weakens fit:

- if framed mainly as producing reports,
- if the technical uncertainty is not clearly stated,
- if the work relies on Canadian corporation/IP ownership in a way that muddies U.S. eligibility,
- if the current 51% Canadian-resident ownership structure is carried into a U.S. SBIR applicant without eligibility review,
- if no U.S. company exists,
- if the project is too domain-consulting-heavy and not enough R&D.

## Stability of U.S. funding

You said you do not trust U.S. programs to last much more than six months from this point. That is a reasonable emotional read on policy volatility, but SBIR/STTR is more durable than ordinary discretionary AI announcements.

The Small Business Administration announced in April 2026 that SBIR/STTR had been reauthorized through **September 30, 2031**. That does not guarantee any one agency topic, review timeline, or AI priority will remain stable, but it means the core program authority is not currently a six-month-only opportunity.

Practical interpretation:

- **SBIR/STTR itself:** relatively durable through 2031.
- **Specific AI topics or agency priorities:** volatile.
- **Application timelines:** slow enough that relying on one grant as near-term income is risky.
- **Best use:** non-dilutive R&D support if the product direction is already coherent.

## Recommended Funding Strategy

## Phase 1: Prepare The Canadian Funding Story

- Write a two-page commercialization brief.
- Identify the Canadian entity path.
- Define the technical R&D questions.
- Define the first Canadian buyer/pilot profile.
- Keep evidence of prototype progress and validation conversations.

## Phase 2: Talk To NRC IRAP

NRC IRAP is the best first institutional conversation because advisors can redirect to other programs if IRAP is not the right fit.

## Phase 3: Seek A Canadian Academic / SME Partner

Use Mitacs if a focused research slice emerges:

- legal RAG evaluation,
- detector validation,
- Canadian insurance/legal corpus methods,
- human-in-the-loop report usability.

## Phase 4: Partner-Pilot Path

If a Canadian carrier, broker, MGA, compliance consultancy, or legal/regtech partner shows interest, evaluate Scale AI or a regional development agency.

## Phase 5: Compute Funding Later

Do not chase AI Compute Access Fund until there is a Canadian company, revenue/investor signal, and real compute need.

---

## Bottom Line

Canadian grants are feasible enough to be worth planning for, but they should not be treated as guaranteed money.

The strongest near-term path is:

> Canadian incorporation or partnership + NRC IRAP conversation + targeted Mitacs research slice + buyer validation.

The weakest path is:

> applying for AI grants as a solo, pre-commercial research idea without Canadian operations or a specific pilot.

The project has a credible Canada story because it sits at the intersection of AI, regulatory productivity, financial-services compliance, Canadian-owned IP, and professional risk review. The next job is to turn that story into an eligible Canadian business/project structure.

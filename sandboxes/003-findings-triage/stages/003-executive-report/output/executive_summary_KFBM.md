## Executive Summary

This review provides an analysis of homeowners insurance policy filings, rate manuals, and endorsements submitted to the Kentucky Department of Insurance by the carrier. As a critical examination of internal documents, this review seeks to highlight areas within the policy language that may present potential risks. For a CEO or Chief Claims Officer, understanding these risks is essential—not only for aligning policy language with regulatory expectations but also for optimizing claims management processes and customer satisfaction.

In examining the carrier's filings, we identified 17 notable findings clustered into two primary risk categories: Magic Number / Magic Valuation Terms and Regulatory Mapping Smells. These findings reveal areas where policy terms may unintentionally lead to ambiguity or misinterpretations during claims processing, potentially increasing exposure to disputes or challenges. Such clarity is critical for maintaining regulatory compliance and ensuring that policyholders fully grasp the scope and limitations of their coverage.

There is a significant opportunity here for the carrier to proactively address these issues before they escalate into more substantial concerns. By refining policy language and ensuring alignment with regulatory guidelines, the carrier can mitigate the risk of claims disputes and demonstrate a commitment to transparent, customer-centric practices.

## Risk Patterns Identified

*Patterns detected in your policy filings, rate manuals, and endorsements.*

### Magic Number / Magic Valuation Terms

**SMELL2-H001** (6 finding(s))

*Language flagged: "Reasonable"*

The pattern identified as SMELL2-H001 under "Magic Number / Magic Valuation Terms" reveals a significant area of concern within your policy documents. Specifically, the use of undefined timing language, such as 'reasonable time,' in loss settlement conditions creates ambiguity about when a claim qualifies for settlement at replacement cost versus actual cash value. This ambiguous language is evident in your policy filings, lacking clear definitions that would guide both policyholders and claims handlers in understanding what constitutes a 'reasonable' period for repairs after loss events such as wind or hail damage.

The practical risk associated with this gap in policy language can lead to disputes with policyholders, potential regulatory scrutiny, and costly litigation. For instance, if a homeowner delays storm-related repairs due to personal circumstances, they risk having their repair costs disputed if the insurer deems the time taken 'unreasonable.' This pattern of undefined terms echoes broader industry issues, such as the labor depreciation class-action wave that highlighted significant carrier liabilities due to vague policy language, resulting in costly settlements like the 15.6 million State Farm ACV case. Additionally, this language increases exposure to bad-faith claims, particularly in states like Kentucky, where courts are inclined to interpret ambiguities against the drafter. These examples underscore the importance of precise policy language to mitigate legal and financial risks.

**SMELL2-H003** (5 finding(s))

*Language flagged: "Actual Cash Value", "REPLACEMENT COST", "Replacement Cost"*

The SMELL2-H003 pattern identifies a critical gap in the policy language of your filings, specifically concerning the usage of valuation terms like 'actual cash value' and 'replacement cost.' What is notably absent in these instances is a clearly defined calculation methodology or an external reference that policyholders can rely on. This absence of precise definitions and methodologies in your policy documents could lead to ambiguities during loss settlement.

From a practical standpoint, the lack of clarity around how 'actual cash value' is determined poses significant risks, potentially leading to disputes and regulatory challenges. For example, past class-action lawsuits, such as the 15.6 million State Farm settlement, highlight the severe financial implications of undefined ACV calculations. Additionally, the labor depreciation lawsuits, which have resulted in hundreds of millions in industry exposure, demonstrate the substantial costs that can arise from unclear policy language. These incidents underscore the importance of clearly defining valuation terms to mitigate the risk of costly disputes and bad-faith claims.

### Regulatory Mapping Smells

**SMELL5-H004** (4 finding(s))

The SMELL5-H004 pattern, identified under "Regulatory Mapping Smells" in your policy filings, highlights a concern where rate-setting and premium-computation filings lack identifiable citations to relevant Kentucky statutes (KRS), administrative regulations (KAR), or Department of Insurance (DOI) bulletins. This gap renders the regulatory authority for these rate rules unverifiable from the filing documentation. For instance, in your filings, there are rate-setting rules that cannot be traced back to any specific legal authority, which raises questions about the legitimacy and compliance of the rate determinations made.

The practical risk associated with this gap is significant and multifaceted. Without clear regulatory backing, there could be an increased likelihood of disputes arising from policyholders who claim discounts or rate adjustments, like those based on a Homeowner Risk Factor, only to encounter inconsistencies in claims handling. This regulatory oversight can lead to added scrutiny and potential fines from state insurance commissioners, similar to the substantial financial penalties imposed during market conduct examinations in states like Florida and Louisiana. In Florida, for example, a 2.575 million fine was assessed across ten carriers due to regulatory gaps, including missing legal citations and compliance oversights during filings. Such situations not only strain relations with policyholders but can also attract regulatory actions, imposing costs both financially and reputationally.

**SMELL5-H005** (1 finding(s))

The SMELL5-H005 pattern under "Regulatory Mapping Smells" highlights instances in policy documents where mandatory coverage requirements are asserted without citing the regulatory authority that mandates such requirements. This pattern is evident in your policy filings, specifically when language suggests certain forms must automatically adjust coverage limits annually without clearly indicating which legal rule necessitates this adjustment. This omission poses a risk of creating ambiguity for policyholders who might question whether these increases are indeed supported by state regulations or if they have been mistakenly promised benefits not rooted in legal mandates or precedents.

The practical risk of this pattern could manifest as disputes when policyholders, relying on the documented promises of automatic coverage increases, find their claims denied due to the absence of regulatory support. Such scenarios expose your organization to significant challenges, including potential legal disputes with policyholders and subsequent regulatory scrutiny. As seen in past events, similar gaps in regulatory compliance have resulted in substantial fines: the Florida OIR imposed 2.575 million in penalties across ten carriers, and Louisiana proposed fines nearing 1 million due to failures in regulatory mapping. These examples underscore the importance of ensuring that policy language is both clear and appropriately linked to authoritative regulations to mitigate financial and reputational risks.

**SMELL5-H006** (1 finding(s))

The SMELL5-H006 pattern under "Regulatory Mapping Smells" highlights a critical issue in your policy documents: sections detailing the loss settlement methodology lack traceable regulatory citations. Specifically, an example of this can be seen in your policy language concerning manufactured homes, where coverage limits are not adjusted for inflation. This methodology appears misaligned with applicable state laws, creating potential confusion over how claims will be settled, particularly regarding inflation adjustments. Such ambiguity could frustrate policyholders who might expect coverage that reflects changes in inflation.

The absence of clear regulatory alignment in your filings exposes your organization to practical risks, including potential claims disputes and regulatory action. If policyholders with manufactured homes anticipate coverage limits adjusting with inflation and are instead faced with settlement methodologies that don't account for such changes, disputes are likely to ensue. This gap also heightens the risk of regulatory scrutiny, similar to what was observed in Florida and Louisiana, where carriers incurred substantial fines — 2.575 million and nearly 1 million, respectively — for missing regulatory compliance in their claims processes. Such precedents underline the financial and reputational costs of allowing similar regulatory ambiguities to persist.

*The patterns identified in this analysis are not theoretical. The following public events illustrate the cost range when similar gaps are not caught early.*

**Magic Number / Magic Valuation Terms**

- **Labor depreciation class-action wave** — A wave of class actions beginning around 2015 alleged that carriers were systematically depreciating labor costs when calculating actual cash value — a practice not disclosed or defined in policy language. One Trumbull/Hartford suit alone alleged more than 5 million in underpayments; industry-wide exposure has been estimated in the hundreds of millions.
- **State Farm ACV settlement** — State Farm reached a 15.6 million settlement in a dispute over how actual cash value was calculated on property claims — a direct consequence of undefined ACV methodology in policy language.
- **Kentucky ambiguity and bad-faith exposure** — Project research (Pedicini example) shows that undefined payment terminology in Kentucky policy language has survived as the basis for bad-faith exposure. Kentucky courts treat policy ambiguity against the drafter.

- **Florida OIR hurricane market conduct exams** — Following post-hurricane market conduct examinations, Florida's OIR levied 2.575 million in fines across ten carriers. Findings included missing required notices, claim handling deadline failures, and payment timing violations — all traceable to regulatory mapping gaps in carrier filings.
- **Louisiana hurricane market conduct exams** — Louisiana proposed nearly 1 million in fines following hurricane claims examinations — the same pattern as Florida, driven by missing state-specific regulatory compliance in carrier claim handling.
- **South Dakota Farmers/Foremost market conduct exam** — A single property insurance market conduct examination produced a 750,000 penalty — establishing the low end of the documented fine range for regulatory mapping failures.

## What Forward-Looking Carriers Are Doing

Carriers that recognize the significance of these findings understand that proactive risk management is key to maintaining both competitive advantage and consumer trust. Forward-thinking companies are taking initiative by revisiting and revising their policy language to ensure clarity and consistency. By clearly defining valuation terms like "actual cash value" and "replacement cost" and explicitly anchoring their rate methodologies, they reduce ambiguities that can lead to disputes and regulatory scrutiny. Incorporating defined timing language in loss settlement conditions further strengthens policy coherence. Such adjustments not only align with best practices but demonstrate a commitment to transparency and customer satisfaction, ultimately fortifying their reputation in the marketplace.

Beyond addressing specific risk patterns, a systematic review of policy language serves as a cornerstone for effective risk management strategies. Regular evaluations allow carriers to adapt to evolving industry standards and regulatory changes, fostering resilience in an ever-shifting landscape. This practice not only minimizes potential exposures but also positions carriers as leaders in adopting policy innovations that enhance service and trust with policyholders.

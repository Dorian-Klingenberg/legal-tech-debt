# Kentucky Homeowners Insurance: Policy Language Risk Patterns

*Industry Analysis — June 2026*  
*Based on review of homeowners policy filings, rate manuals, and endorsements from two Kentucky carriers*

---

## Executive Summary

This briefing presents an industry-wide analysis of homeowners insurance policy language within Kentucky, focusing on potential risk patterns that could affect your decision-making and strategic planning. The research is not a compliance audit of specific companies but a broader evaluation of the policy filings, rate manuals, and endorsements submitted by two carriers to the Kentucky Department of Insurance. We have identified 25 confirmed findings classified into three key risk categories: Magic Number / Magic Valuation Terms, Calculation Rule Drift / Unversioned Rate Reference, and Regulatory Mapping Smells. Understanding these patterns is crucial for Chief Claims Officers and CEOs to refine risk management practices and ensure policy language aligns with evolving regulatory landscapes.

The most significant finding of this analysis is the presence of three specific risk patterns that appear independently across both carriers, suggesting these are industry norms rather than isolated incidents. These patterns primarily involve inconsistencies in valuation terms, drift in calculation rules, and potential mismaps to regulatory requirements. Such patterns underscore systemic vulnerabilities that could have industry-wide ramifications, influencing how coverage is interpreted and claims are processed.

For a forward-thinking carrier eager to mitigate these risks, proactively revisiting and refining policy language, rate rules, and endorsements can offer a competitive edge. By addressing these industry patterns now, carriers can enhance policy clarity, reduce potential conflicts during claims processing, and fortify regulatory compliance, ultimately improving customer trust and operational efficiency.

## Industry-Wide Patterns

*Patterns appearing in both carriers independently carry the strongest signal — they reflect industry drafting conventions, not isolated filing choices.*

### Magic Number / Magic Valuation Terms

**SMELL2-H003** (5 + 5 findings across both carriers)

*Language flagged: "Actual Cash Value", "REPLACEMENT COST", "Replacement Cost"*

The pattern known as SMELL2-H003 highlights a significant issue in the use of valuation terms like "actual cash value" and "replacement cost" within policy documents. The policy language reviewed for two carriers indicates a lack of clarity, often omitting any calculation methodology or external reference for these terms. For instance, "market value" is mentioned without explaining how it is determined, leaving policyholders unsure of how their property's value will be assessed after a loss. Such gaps in policy definitions were found equally across the analyzed carriers, raising concerns about transparency and understanding for the policyholders.

This omission of clear valuation methodologies presents a substantial risk for dispute and regulatory challenges. When policyholders and insurers have different interpretations of terms like "market value," there is a heightened potential for claim disputes that could escalate to litigation. Historical cases underscore this risk; for example, a State Farm settlement of 15.6 million arose precisely from vague ACV calculations. Similarly, the labor depreciation class-action wave, with its massive financial ramifications, was rooted in undefined policy language regarding calculation methodologies. The ambiguity not only jeopardizes customer satisfaction but also opens up carriers to significant financial and reputational harm, as seen with past industry-wide exposures.

### Regulatory Mapping Smells

**SMELL5-H004** (1 + 4 findings across both carriers)

The SMELL5-H004 pattern, under the "Regulatory Mapping Smells," identifies a critical omission in the rate-setting and premium-computation filings by insurance carriers. This pattern specifically flags instances where these filings do not reference any relevant Kentucky statute (KRS), administrative regulation (KAR), or Department of Insurance (DOI) bulletin, leaving the regulatory foundation of the proposed rate changes unverifiable. Both carriers analyzed show evidence of this issue, indicating a recurring problem in ensuring compliance with state-mandated regulatory standards. Essentially, the filings lack the necessary citations that would authenticate the legal basis for the rates being applied.

This gap presents a significant risk of disputes and regulatory exposure for insurers. When rate justifications are not clearly traceable to appropriate regulatory sources, policyholders facing a claim could potentially challenge the legitimacy of their premium calculations or payouts. For instance, if a policyholder disputes a decreased claim payout due to unsubstantiated rate rules, carriers might find themselves in protracted disputes or litigation. Historical precedents, such as the 2.575 million fines imposed by Florida following post-hurricane market conduct exams, underscore the financial and reputational fallout from similar regulatory mapping gaps. Louisiana and South Dakota have faced comparable situations, with fines demonstrating the substantial implications of unchecked regulatory compliance deficiencies.

**SMELL5-H005** (1 + 1 findings across both carriers)

The regulatory mapping issue identified as SMELL5-H005 highlights a critical gap within policy documents from the carriers analyzed. This pattern pertains to instances where mandatory coverages are asserted as being required without the necessary regulatory citations to support such claims. The absence of clear regulatory backing can lead to ambiguity, leaving both carriers and policyholders uncertain about the exact nature of the mandated coverage. In the findings, each carrier displayed one such issue in their policy, raising concerns about the clarity and enforceability of the policy terms.

The practical risk of this omission is multifaceted, encompassing potential disputes between policyholders and insurers and exposing carriers to regulatory scrutiny. For example, policyholders may anticipate certain coverage to be guaranteed, only to face claim denials when such assurances lack regulatory grounding. This can lead to contentious disputes, similar to those seen in broader industry examples where unclear regulatory compliance has resulted in significant fines. Florida and Louisiana have levied substantial fines, 2.575 million and nearly 1 million respectively, for issues linked to regulatory mapping gaps post-hurricane events. Such exposures not only tarnish reputations but also carry hefty financial penalties, underscoring the importance of transparent and well-documented policy provisions.

## Risk Context: What This Costs When It Goes Wrong

*The patterns identified in this analysis are not theoretical. The following public events illustrate the cost range when similar gaps are not caught early.*

**Magic Number / Magic Valuation Terms**

- **Labor depreciation class-action wave** — A wave of class actions beginning around 2015 alleged that carriers were systematically depreciating labor costs when calculating actual cash value — a practice not disclosed or defined in policy language. One Trumbull/Hartford suit alone alleged more than 5 million in underpayments; industry-wide exposure has been estimated in the hundreds of millions.
- **State Farm ACV settlement** — State Farm reached a 15.6 million settlement in a dispute over how actual cash value was calculated on property claims — a direct consequence of undefined ACV methodology in policy language.
- **Kentucky ambiguity and bad-faith exposure** — Project research (Pedicini example) shows that undefined payment terminology in Kentucky policy language has survived as the basis for bad-faith exposure. Kentucky courts treat policy ambiguity against the drafter.

**Calculation Rule Drift / Unversioned Rate Reference**

- **Marshall Fire underinsurance** — A single home insured for 419,000 faced an 850,000 rebuild estimate after the Marshall Fire — a 431,000 gap driven by replacement cost estimates that were not synchronized with actual post-loss reconstruction pricing.
- **Han v. State Farm** — Alleged systematic property underpayment from using lower new-construction pricing rather than reconstruction pricing. Pattern affects entire claim population, not individual cases.

**Regulatory Mapping Smells**

- **Florida OIR hurricane market conduct exams** — Following post-hurricane market conduct examinations, Florida's OIR levied 2.575 million in fines across ten carriers. Findings included missing required notices, claim handling deadline failures, and payment timing violations — all traceable to regulatory mapping gaps in carrier filings.
- **Louisiana hurricane market conduct exams** — Louisiana proposed nearly 1 million in fines following hurricane claims examinations — the same pattern as Florida, driven by missing state-specific regulatory compliance in carrier claim handling.
- **South Dakota Farmers/Foremost market conduct exam** — A single property insurance market conduct examination produced a 750,000 penalty — establishing the low end of the documented fine range for regulatory mapping failures.

## What Forward-Looking Carriers Are Doing

To proactively manage risk in light of these identified patterns, a forward-looking carrier would begin by conducting a thorough review of their policy language. This review would focus on clarifying undefined terms like "actual cash value" and "replacement cost" to align with industry standards and avoid ambiguity. It would also involve anchoring the rate methodology clearly within the policy to provide transparency and consistency. Additionally, carriers would benefit from specifying timing language in loss settlement processes to eliminate uncertainties and potential disputes. By addressing these areas, a carrier not only enhances policyholder trust but also strengthens its market position by reducing the likelihood of unexpected claims disputes.

Engaging in systematic policy language review is a prudent practice that extends beyond immediate risk management. By continuously improving policy clarity and aligning with evolving regulatory standards, carriers can maintain a competitive edge while fostering stronger relationships with regulators and customers alike. This ongoing diligence not only mitigates current risks but also positions a carrier well for future challenges in an ever-changing insurance landscape.
